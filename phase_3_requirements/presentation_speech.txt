SLIDE 1 — COVER
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Good [morning/afternoon], everyone. Thank you for the time. Today I want to
introduce a technology that solves a very specific but growing problem: how do
you keep a whole team of robots on a construction site reliably connected — all
the time, without anyone lifting a finger. Our approach is an automatic WiFi
mesh-to-cellular failover system that keeps those robots coordinated below a
two-hundred-millisecond budget. Let me walk you through the idea, how it works,
and where it's headed.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 2 — CONTENTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Here's the path I'll take you through. First, the problem — why a single network
just isn't enough on a jobsite. Then our solution and the core technology behind
it, the architecture, and how a handover actually works. After that, what makes
us different, what we've already validated, the market and business model, and
finally the roadmap. I'll keep it tight.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 3 — THE PROBLEM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Construction sites are increasingly run by teams of robots that have to behave
like one coordinated system — not isolated machines. That coordination needs
near-real-time sync, under two hundred milliseconds. The catch is that no single
on-site network delivers that. A WiFi mesh is fast and cheap, but it fades the
moment you put concrete and steel between nodes. Cellular covers the whole site,
but it's slower and expensive to lean on constantly. And when the link drops,
it's not a minor glitch — the fleet loses coordination, TCP sessions die and have
to restart, someone has to manually reconfigure the network, and safety-critical
work stops. Our insight is simple: use WiFi where it's strong, cellular where it
isn't, and switch fast enough that the robot never notices.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 4 — OUR SOLUTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
So we built a network that switches itself. Each robot keeps one continuous
identity, and a hybrid controller quietly decides which link carries its traffic.
Four things fall out of that. The robot is always connected — the system picks
the link, never the operator. Failover is seamless — traffic slides to cellular
as WiFi fades and back when it recovers, with the session intact. Every switch is
measured per robot against that latency target. And it's technology-neutral — the
exact same controller runs over LTE or 5G. We deliver this two ways: as an
embedded module inside the gateway the robot already carries, and as an
operator-facing assurance layer for fleet-wide reporting.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 5 — CORE TECHNOLOGY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Here's the engine. The controller re-checks every robot twice a second, and it
makes decisions on four principles. First, dual-signal fusion — it blends a
smoothed RSSI signal with a one-second packet-delivery ratio, so a momentary
radio dip alone never triggers a needless switch. Second, hysteresis — the
threshold to come back to WiFi sits three decibels above the one to leave it, and
has to hold for two checks, so it never oscillates at the boundary. Third, a
staleness guard — if it hears no WiFi frame for three seconds, it fails over
anyway, catching slow fades the thresholds would miss. And fourth,
make-before-break routing — the new path is installed before the old one is
dropped, so a live TCP session survives the handover. On the right you can see
the actual tuned values.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 6 — ARCHITECTURE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Architecturally, the trick is on the right: one service IP, two paths. We have a
four-node 802.11s mesh with dual-band access points, and a single macro cell —
LTE or 5G. Both paths lead to the same destination address. That's the key — it
means a handover is just an internal routing change, not a reconnection to a new
endpoint, which is exactly why sessions survive. And we model propagation with
one building-aware loss model applied consistently across WiFi, LTE, and 5G on a
fixed four-hundred-by-four-hundred-meter site, so the comparison between
technologies is genuinely like-for-like.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 7 — HANDOVER LOGIC
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Every cycle the controller does four things: it senses signal, delivery, and time
since the last frame; it decides using the thresholds and hysteresis; it acts
with make-before-break rerouting; and it records the switch and measures the
interruption. The decision rules are on the right. Notice they're deliberately
asymmetric — it's easy to leave WiFi when things degrade, but hard to come back;
you need a stronger, sustained recovery. That bias keeps the system on the
preferred WiFi link whenever it genuinely can, without ever flapping between the
two.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 8 — DIFFERENTIATORS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
So what makes this different from off-the-shelf multipath solutions? Four things.
One — we do joint, environment-tuned switching using both signal and delivery,
and we validate it against real access-point hardware profiles, not idealized
radios. Two — we give a measurable latency guarantee: a quantified per-switch
interruption against a two-hundred-millisecond budget, which is the number a
robotics customer actually cares about. Three — we run a genuine head-to-head of
LTE versus 5G in the same role and topology, which almost no public study does
for this use case. And four — it's session-preserving by design, keeping
interruption in the millisecond range instead of a full timeout-and-retry.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 9 — VALIDATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
This isn't just a concept. Phase One is complete — as of March 2026 we've built
and run a full NS-3 comparison of WiFi-plus-LTE against WiFi-plus-5G across
multiple seeds and node counts. The controller is validated, the measurement
pipeline logs every switch, and we scaled it to twenty mobile robots that all
switched automatically in both directions. Critically, every client held under
two hundred milliseconds across all runs, and we confirmed reliable return from
cellular back to WiFi — not just the outbound failover. I'll be honest about the
limits: it hasn't been field-tested on hardware yet, and we've got more
simulation planned to strengthen the statistics.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 10 — MARKET & MODEL
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
On the opportunity — we sit at the intersection of two fast-growing markets.
Construction robotics is heading from about six and a half billion dollars to
over fifteen billion by 2030, and private 5G is growing even faster, toward a
hundred and fifty billion by 2033. The driver is concrete: the US alone needed
around five hundred and forty-six thousand more construction workers in 2023, and
the same shortage exists in Germany, Japan, and Korea — so automation isn't
optional. We monetize three ways: licensing the controller IP to gateway makers
for early revenue, a recurring connectivity-assurance subscription, and one-time
integration fees with telecom partners.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 11 — ROADMAP
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Here's the path forward. The foundation — simulation of the controller and
measurement pipeline — is done. We're now in enhanced simulation: realistic
waypoint mobility, QoS traffic, mesh handover modeling, and more statistical
rigor. After that, lab validation on a real hardware testbed, then a field trial
on a live site to prove seamless failover and the latency target in the real
world. Commercially, over one to three years, we file the IP, license the
controller to gateway makers and integrators, and layer the assurance service on
top.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SLIDE 12 — CLOSING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
To sum up — the whole point is keeping robot fleets connected without anyone
noticing the switch. We fuse two signals with hysteresis so switches are never
false or flapping; we preserve live sessions through make-before-break; we've hit
the sub-two-hundred-millisecond target with up to twenty robots; and we've done
the rare direct LTE-versus-5G comparison for this domain. Phase One is validated,
the lab and field work is mapped out, and the path to market is clear. Thank you
— I'd be glad to take your questions.
